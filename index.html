Our Mission

Clean air starts with understanding it

AM for PM works to increase worldwide understanding of airborne particulate matter and its impacts on human health and the environment.

Why it matters: You might be wondering, what is so wrong with pollution? Answer: basically everything. It can impact every scale of society, from individual well-being to a country's economy. Some of the most harmful air pollutants are nitrogen dioxide, ozone, and especially particulate matter (PM). Fine particles like PM2.5 are among the most harmful air pollutants, yet remain widely misunderstood.

How it works: PM2.5 breathed = concentration × how much air you breathe × time. We use typical breathing rates (about 0.3 m³ per hour asleep, 0.5 m³ awake and resting, and 1 to 3 m³ when active), assume indoor air has about 60% of the outdoor level plus any indoor sources, and compare your day with the same day at the WHO guideline of 5 µg/m³. The basics

Understanding Particulate Matter

Particulate matter is a mixture of solid particles and liquid droplets suspended in the air. Made out of particles such as organic chemical compounds, soot, heavy metals, sulfates, nitrates, and acids, PM2.5 and other PMs can dangerously alter our well-being and future lifestyles.

Common size groups are PM10, PM2.5, and PM1.0. The number is the particle's width in micrometers (µm); one micrometer is a thousandth of a millimeter.

Sources

Vehicles, power plants, factories, construction, agriculture, wildfires, and indoor sources like cooking and burning candles.

Sizes

PM10: coarse dust, pollen and mold. PM2.5: fine particles, about 30 times thinner than a human hair. Ultrafine: smaller than 0.1 µm.

Exposure

The smaller the particle, the deeper it travels. PM10 mostly stops in the nose and throat; PM2.5 reaches the deepest parts of the lungs and can even enter the bloodstream.

Want the full story? Read our PM2.5 Guide for how fine particles form, travel through the body, and affect the environment.

The complete guide

PM2.5: the invisible pollutant

PM2.5 is the name for airborne particles 2.5 micrometers wide or smaller. They are too small to see one by one, small enough to slip past the body's defenses, and responsible for more health harm than any other air pollutant.

1 · What is it?

Smaller than you can imagine

The "2.5" means 2.5 micrometers (µm). A human hair is roughly 50 to 70 µm wide, so you could line up about 20 to 30 PM2.5 particles across the width of a single hair. Many are far smaller still.

One particle is invisible, but billions together create the gray-brown haze you see on smoggy or smoky days. PM2.5 is not one chemical. It is a mix of soot (black carbon), dust, metals, sulfates, nitrates, ammonium, and organic compounds, and the recipe changes from place to place and season to season.

Because they are so light, fine particles can stay in the air for days to weeks and ride the wind for hundreds or even thousands of miles. That is how wildfire smoke from one region can turn the sky orange in another.

2 · Where it comes from

Made directly, and made in the air

Primary PM2.5

Released straight into the air

Soot from diesel and gasoline engines, smoke from wildfires, wood stoves and burning trash, dust from construction and roads, and particles from cooking.

Secondary PM2.5

Formed in the air from gases

Gases like sulfur dioxide (from coal power plants), nitrogen oxides (from vehicles), ammonia (from farms) and volatile organic compounds react in sunlight and turn into new particles. In many places this is a large share of the PM2.5 people breathe.

Transportation

Cars, trucks, buses, ships and planes. Levels are often highest near busy roads and idling vehicles, such as school pickup lines.

Power and industry

Power plants, factories, refineries and mining release particles and the gases that form them.

Fires

Wildfires, agricultural burning and home wood burning. Wildfire smoke is a growing share of PM2.5 in the U.S. as fire seasons get longer.

Indoors

Frying and searing food, gas stoves, candles, incense, fireplaces, vaping and smoking. Indoor levels can spike higher than outdoors.

3 · The journey into your body

Why size matters so much

Your nose, throat and airways are good at trapping big particles. PM2.5 is small enough to get past almost all of those defenses.

4 · Health effects

From head to toe

Air pollution is the world's second-leading risk factor for early death, after high blood pressure, and it contributes to about 1 in 4 heart disease deaths and over 1 in 4 dementia deaths worldwide (State of Global Air 2025).

Lungs

Triggers asthma attacks and coughing, worsens COPD and bronchitis, reduces lung function, and raises the risk of lung cancer, even in people who have never smoked.

Heart and blood vessels

Linked to heart attacks, strokes, irregular heartbeat and high blood pressure. Heart disease is the largest single cause of PM2.5-related deaths.

Brain

Long-term exposure is linked to dementia and cognitive decline in older adults, and researchers are studying effects on children's learning and attention.

Pregnancy and babies

Exposure during pregnancy is linked to preterm birth and low birth weight, which can affect a child's health for life.

Short-term (hours to days)

On a smoky or smoggy day

  • Itchy eyes, sore throat, coughing
  • Asthma attacks and shortness of breath
  • More emergency room visits and hospital stays
  • Higher risk of heart attack and stroke in vulnerable people
Long-term (months to years)

Breathing polluted air year after year

  • Slower lung growth in children
  • Development of asthma and chronic lung disease
  • Heart disease, lung cancer and dementia
  • Shorter life expectancy
5 · Who's most at risk

Pollution doesn't affect everyone equally

Children and teens

Their lungs are still growing, they breathe more air for their body size than adults, and they spend more time active outdoors.

Older adults

More likely to have heart or lung conditions that pollution makes worse.

People with health conditions

Asthma, COPD, heart disease and diabetes all raise the risk, as does pregnancy.

Communities near pollution

Neighborhoods beside highways, ports, factories and power plants often breathe the most pollution, and are often lower-income communities.

6 · Effects on the environment

It harms the planet, too

Haze

Fine particles scatter and absorb light, blurring views in cities and in national parks.

Water and soil

Particles settle onto land and water. Sulfates and nitrates can make lakes and streams more acidic, upset nutrient balance and strip nutrients from soil.

Plants and crops

Dust and acid deposits can damage forests and farm crops and reduce how much sunlight reaches leaves.

Climate and ice

Black carbon (soot) absorbs sunlight and warms the air. When it lands on snow and glaciers, it darkens them so they melt faster.

A dangerous loop: a warming climate brings hotter, drier conditions and more wildfires, and wildfires release huge amounts of PM2.5 and soot, which add to warming.

7 · How it's measured

Reading the numbers

PM2.5 is measured in micrograms per cubic meter (µg/m³): the weight of particles in a box of air one meter on each side. Government monitors, satellites and low-cost sensors all measure it. To make the numbers easy to understand, the U.S. turns them into the color-coded Air Quality Index (AQI).

8 · Myths vs facts

Clearing the air

Myth:If the sky looks clear, the air is clean.
Fact:PM2.5 is invisible at moderate levels. The air can look perfectly clear and still be unhealthy. Check the AQI instead of the sky.
Myth:Staying indoors always protects you.
Fact:Outdoor particles leak inside, and cooking, candles and gas stoves add more. Indoors is only safer if you keep windows closed and filter the air.
Myth:Any face mask will filter smoke.
Fact:Cloth and surgical masks let most fine particles through. A well-fitted N95 or KN95 respirator filters most PM2.5.
Myth:Air pollution is only a big-city problem.
Fact:Wildfire smoke, farm burning and wood stoves affect rural areas too, and smoke can travel thousands of miles.
Myth:Low levels of PM2.5 are perfectly safe.
Fact:Scientists have not found a level with zero health risk. That is why the WHO lowered its guideline to 5 µg/m³ in 2021 and the EPA tightened its standard in 2024.
Myth:One person can't make a difference.
Fact:Turning off an idling car, skipping a backyard burn, or teaching others to check the AQI all reduce exposure. Clean air rules, driven by public support, have cut U.S. particle pollution a lot since the 1970s.
9 · Protect yourself

Simple steps that work

Protect your health

  • Check the AQI each morning, especially before sports or recess
  • Move hard exercise indoors on orange, red or purple days
  • Wear a well-fitted N95 if you must be out in smoke
  • Run a HEPA air purifier in the room where you sleep
  • Use a range hood or open a window when cooking

Reduce pollution

  • Turn the engine off instead of idling
  • Walk, bike, carpool or take transit when you can
  • Skip burning leaves and trash
  • Choose electric tools and appliances where possible
  • Share what you learn: awareness is the first step

DIY air purifier: a "Corsi-Rosenthal box" (four MERV-13 furnace filters taped to a box fan) is a low-cost way to clean the air in a room. Many schools have built them as a class project.

10 · Glossary

Words to know

Micrometer (µm)
One millionth of a meter, or one thousandth of a millimeter. PM2.5 particles are 2.5 µm or smaller.
µg/m³
Micrograms per cubic meter: the weight of particles in one cubic meter of air. This is how PM2.5 is measured.
AQI (Air Quality Index)
A color-coded 0–500 scale that turns pollution measurements into simple health advice. Higher numbers mean dirtier air.
Alveoli
Millions of tiny air sacs deep in the lungs where oxygen passes into the blood. PM2.5 can reach them.
Black carbon
The sooty part of PM2.5 from burning fuels and wood. It harms health and warms the climate.
Secondary particles
Particles that form in the air when gases like sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides and ammonia react.
HEPA filter
High-Efficiency Particulate Air filter. A true HEPA filter captures at least 99.97% of particles 0.3 µm in size.
MERV rating
A 1–16 scale for furnace and HVAC filters. MERV-13 or higher captures most fine particles.
NAAQS
National Ambient Air Quality Standards: the legal pollution limits the U.S. EPA sets under the Clean Air Act.

Science experiment

Be an Air Detective

Make a particle catcher

You need: 3 white index cards, petroleum jelly, string or tape, a magnifying glass, and a grown-up helper.

  1. Spread a thin layer of petroleum jelly on one side of each card.
  2. Hang the cards in 3 places: near a road, in a park, and inside your house.
  3. Wait 3 days without touching them.
  4. Look at each card with a magnifying glass. Which one caught the most specks?

What did you find?

The specks you can see are bigger particles like dust, pollen and soot. PM2.5 is too tiny to see, even with a magnifying glass. Scientists use special sensors to count them!

Think about it: Which place was dirtiest? Why do you think so? Draw a picture of your cards and share it with your class.
